\section{Receiving over Lightning}\label{sec:receive}

A user with no on-chain funds receives a Lightning payment and wants a
balance it can leave with alone. An Ark client gets an out-of-round
output co-signed by the operator that expires with its round, or, below
the operator's floor, a ledger credit with no on-chain claim. The tree
takes the same payment with less trust.

\paragraph{Cumulative hash-locked vouchers.} A \tr{CLAIM}$^{h}$ voucher
(Appendix~\ref{app:claim}) is the operator's signature over a source leaf
it owns, a claim leaf for the recipient and a payment hash; whoever holds
it and the preimage can redeem it alone. The recipient generates the
preimage and issues the invoice, the payer's HTLC reaches the operator,
the operator issues a voucher under that hash, and the recipient reveals
the preimage so the operator can settle. Nothing reaches the chain. Since
a redemption advances the source leaf's $\mathsf{seq}$ and voids every
other voucher against it, repeated receives are one \emph{cumulative}
voucher that the operator reissues for the running total; the recipient
keeps the latest, and needs no contest window because only it can redeem
and it never prefers a smaller state.

\paragraph{Settlement.} The voucher is redeemed only if the operator
fails to settle it. Normally the operator
puts the claim leaf into the next cohort graft at about one vbyte per
recipient (\S\ref{sec:graft}) and retires the voucher by advancing the
credit leaf's $\mathsf{seq}$: one \tr{UPDATE} each ($1{,}030\vB$), or
one \tr{REWRITE}$_h$ for a subtree of credit leaves at about $133\vB$
per changed leaf and $16\vB$ more under the exit-consent form (measured
at $h = 6$, \S\ref{sec:batching}).
Unilaterally the recipient redeems one \tr{CLAIM}$^{h}$: $1{,}609\vB$% TODO(0.10.0 sizes): CLAIM^h grows with tree_id and the carve constraint.
 at $D = 16$, about 16{,}100\,sat at 10\,sat/vB, no timelock, against
1{,}311 to $2{,}511\vB$ and a timelock to unroll an Ark round tree of 256
to 65{,}536 members. The redemption
proves two positions; if the credit leaf's sibling slot is reserved for
the recipient's claim leaf, a sibling-pair \tr{CLAIM} shares fifteen of
sixteen levels and costs about $1{,}130\vB$ (estimate, not built). Per
receive nothing reaches the chain, there is no floor beyond dust at
redemption, nothing expires, and the recipient need not be online.

\paragraph{What the recipient trusts.} The covenant stops the operator
from redeeming more than the source holds or paying anyone but the
recipient. It does not stop over-issuance or voiding (advancing
$\mathsf{seq}$ before the recipient redeems). A credit leaf dedicated to
one recipient removes over-issuance, since every voucher against it goes
to that recipient, who can watch its amount. Of the operator's ways to
void, \tr{UPDATE}$^{x}$ (the exit key, which is the recipient's, must
sign), exact payouts and a NUMS evictor close three; the fourth, a
voucher the operator issues itself and redeems with plain \tr{CLAIM}, is
closed by \tr{CLAIM}$^{x}$, which requires a signature by the source
leaf's exit key (Appendix~\ref{app:claim}). With the three variants in
the taptree a credit leaf can only grow or pay its recipient, whose
exposure is the operator's liveness. \tr{CLAIM}$^{x}$ and its
hash-locked form \tr{CLAIM}$^{hx}$ are built in the FULL tier, at 8 bytes
of script over the plain forms and 64 of witness, and the test suite runs
the credit-leaf case: the operator's own voucher with its own
transaction signature fails. The Lean model is unaffected, since
\lean{Claim} carries an opaque authorisation. Where one
operator leaf backs many recipients, over-issuance is equivocation with a
compact proof, which a slashable bond can deter; voiding has no compact
proof, so no bond reaches it.

The credit is one-way: if the recipient could also spend, an older,
larger voucher would be a stale state needing a window to supersede, as
in every bidirectional channel. For spending, the recipient redeems into
a channel leaf (\S\ref{sec:channels}).
